Pink Floyd - The Piper at the Gates of Dawn

Pink Floyd's first album, the only recorded fully with should-have-been-icon Syd Barrett, is a long way from Dark Side of the Moon. Pink Floyd, who were then a psychedelic rock group, rather than a progressive rock one, were handed a record contract by EMI in 1967 and recorded their first album, The Piper at the Gates of Dawn, in Abbey Road studios while the Beatles were next door making Sgt. (fucking) Pepper. And, like most pop/rock groups of the 60s, the band loved a good non-album single. Or maybe only Syd did, because after he left in early 1968, they issued two more singles and then didn't release any (in the UK), not even cuts from albums, until 1979's "Another Brick in the Wall, Pt. 2" (which promptly became that year's Christmas number one). But before all that, their first three UK singles, the only three featuring Barrett, didn't feature on a studio album. So, do we put them on the end of this one? Well…

You see, the problem is that the recording sessions for Pink Floyd's follow-up album, A Saucerful of Secrets, technically began immediately following the release of their debut, with one of the sophomore album's most revered tracks, "Set the Controls for the Heart of the Sun", being recorded just days after the release of Piper. Before this, two of the three singles we're interested in were released: "Arnold Layne" and "See Emily Play". Proper sessions for A Saucerful of Secrets began in October 1967, but there were numerous issues with Barrett's behaviour, and he was eventually kicked out of the group in January 1968 after the band hired his replacement on guitar, David Gilmour. In the end, only three songs of the seven-track album would contain any involvement from Barrett. I bring this up because the band's third single, "Apples and Oranges", was recorded in these autumn sessions with Barrett, but being released in November 1967, it lies closer to August 1967's Piper than it does to June 1968's A Saucerful of Secrets. So even though it was recorded in the sessions for Secrets, many people (including those who compiled Piper's 40th anniversary disc) associate the single more with the debut rather than the follow-up. And so, my decision here of how far to reach in terms of songs to include, is if Barrett is performing on it, it's a Piper-era single, and otherwise it's elsewhere in their discography. This means that the band's first three singles, "Arnold Layne", "See Emily Play", and "Apples and Oranges", all will be put on our disc here, along with a few other tracks.

In that August session that produced "Set the Controls for the Heart of the Sun", the band also recorded "Scream Thy Last Scream", which was then scheduled to be the band's third single, backed with "Vegetable Man", to be released in September '67. However, the record label and the band's management both decided that the songs were too dark for the 7" singles market, and cancelled the single. Neither song saw an official release until The Early Years 1965–1972 box set was released in 2016. Also in that box set was the instrumental "In the Beechwoods", recorded in those Autumn sessions with Barrett, so we'll include that as well. And to make it clear now: this is the limit of what we will be including, even though there's tons of Pink Floyd bootlegs out there, simply because I'm not going through the nebulous job of creating a 3 CD set of every tape Barrett's guitar touched. So, no, "Have You Got It Yet?" will not be on this set.

However, we're not quite done yet. You see, just before Pink Floyd were signed by EMI, they recorded two songs for the film Tonite Let's All Make Love in London in January 1967: "Interstellar Overdrive" and "Nick's Boogie". Only the former, which would eventually end up on Piper in re-recorded form, would be included in the soundtrack. Both songs are long improvisations, more representative of the band's live sound than their short pop songs that they put out on 7". An edited version of "Interstellar Overdrive" was included on the soundtrack album for the film upon its release in 1968, but the full versions of both would eventually be released upon the album's 1990 re-release, and then put onto a dedicated EP titled London '66-'67, released in 1995. Now, I'm largely avoiding including early Pink Floyd recordings from before they had a record deal, but considering the former track would end up on the album, and it's a professional recording of it, I'll let it slide.
