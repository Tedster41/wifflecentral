The KLF - The White Room

This album is part of a series of articles. Go to the previous article?

As a heads up before we begin, out of all of the albums here, this took the longest to research. Also, the CDs will not be necessarily listed in the correct order for the package, but will instead be listed as is relevant to the story. That should be an indicator of the hell that's about to break loose.

The KLF's second album, and fourth overall by members Jimmy Cauty and Bill Drummond, is a very early 90s electronic music album. Featuring some of their already released hits, as well as plenty of new tracks, this album stands close to the cliff-edge that the band members were soon to jump off of. But to explain how we got to that point, we need to go back to the start.

Jimmy Cauty and Bill Drummond began collaborating musically on New Year's Day, 1987. They went under the alias of "The Justified Ancients of Mu Mu", and were a hip hop group who blatantly stole samples without clearance. This would be to their detriment, when ABBA (whose song "Dancing Queen" was used on the group's first album for a solid 45 seconds with barely any additions from the band) forcibly withdrew their first album from sale. After failing to meet with them to discuss terms for getting a licence, they released their second album in early 1988, aptly titled Who Killed The JAMs?. The final track on this album was a six minute song called "Burn the Bastards". This is where the KLF started. Shortly after the release of the album, this song was released as a single, but not as the Justified Ancients of Mu Mu, but as the KLF. However, since this track was released from the Who Killed The JAMs? album, it will not be included on any discs here. However, it's worth mentioning because this song was a movement away from hip hop, and more into a burgeoning form of music, electronic.

Annoyed that people saw them as leading a "a crusade for sampling", Cauty and Drummond decided their next record would have no samples, but would instead feature, in Drummond's words, "pure dance music, without any reference points, without any nod to the history of rock and roll." And, because of their ignorance of all past references to music, carving their own path, they, in this venture, accidentally invented a new subgenre of electronic music. What to call it? Well, the song itself was called "What Time is Love?", but was subtitled "Pure Trance". And thus, 'trance music' was born.

In total, there were four KLF-produced versions of the track, the original "Pure Trance" version and the second "Pure Trance" version on the B-side of the 1988 release, and then on a separate release the following year, the "Primal Remix" and "Techno Slam Mix". But, the KLF were not going to make more "Pure Trance" tracks yet, for they had another idea. The two, in their spare time and with the money they had made from a number one novelty hit they had also made in '88 under the name "The Timelords" (take a gander what song they blatantly ripped off), began work on an ambitious road film called The White Room. They recorded the soundtrack, and had it ready to be a tie-in album for when the film was released. Also around this time, something happened to that old "Pure Trance" single. You see, at first, "What Time is Love" did nothing. It was an experimental electronic track, what do you expect? However, as usual, it was those in Europe, particularly Italy, who picked up the track and started playing it in clubs. Seeing this attention, the group decided to get back into the studio in 1989, not for the film, but to record the sequel to "What Time is Love", which would be titled "3 A.M. Eternal", and also given the "Pure Trance" subtitle and relevant cover art. While it was originally released in May 1989, it was re-released in September with new remixes, including one by Jimmy Cauty's side project, The Orb. They also decided to include the track in their film, and therefore on the soundtrack album for it.

The album itself isn't fully representative of what the film's soundtrack would eventually consist of, as it would include excerpts from their 1990 album Chill Out (including the kinda-single "Madrugada Eterna"), both 1990 and 1991 versions of "Last Train to Trancentral" (which they hadn't written at this point), the "Break for Love" version of "3 A.M. Eternal", rather than the "Pure Trance Original", and the film did not include "Kylie Said to Jason".

However, the group found themselves struggling to complete the film. They were running out of money, and it wasn't finished. To try and climb out of the problem, they released "Kylie Said to Jason", a deliberately commercial and Pet Shop Boys-sounding track, as a single from the album. The song itself was never featured in the film, and was only included on the album so it would sell with the single. The track had multiple B-side remixes, like "Kylie Said Trance" and "Kylie in a Trance". The idea was, off the back of their "Timelords" track from '88, the song would be a success, and like the Timelords before it, fund the project, hopefully this time the rest of the way. But, it didn't. "Kylie Said to Jason" didn't even make the UK singles charts. Defeated, the film was abandoned, and the above album was never officially released. They were both of course leaked, because it's the KLF.

But with the success of "What Time is Love", the band was not down and out just yet. They kept going with the "Pure Trance" series, and in March 1990, released the… 5th single in the series?! Yes, so you see, there is a seemingly endless iceberg of KLF works that never saw the light of day, including "Pure Trance" releases 3 and 4. Pure Trance 3, titled "Love Trance", has apparently been found on an old DAT that Cauty found recently, while 4, "Turn Up the Strobe", is not as easy to pin down. I made the strict restriction that I shouldn't touch any bootlegs or "unreleased" stuff here, aside from film-related works, because I've been down that rabbit hole and I left with more questions than what I had started with, the main one being, "How many of those actually involved the KLF?". So, we're staying away from those, and only talking about Pure Trance 5, "Last Train to Trancentral", "Trancentral" being the colloquial name for the house the group were squatting in (yes really), in the basement of which was their studio. This would be the last track released in the Pure Trance series, and while there were rumours about a release called "E-Train to Trancentral", this never (officially) came about.

Later in 1990, the group put out a new version of "What Time is Love", this time subtitled "Live at Trancentral". This version was very unlike its predecessor, as instead of being a deep, moody progressive work, this featured crowd noise, rapping, and samples! This style of music, dubbed "stadium house" by the group, proved a lot more successful than the original track, reaching number 5 on the UK singles chart. Seeing this success, the band went back to the old White Room tracks and re-recorded them in a style much closer to their new stadium house, such as by adding rapping to some of the tracks. In the process, they re-recorded the next "Pure Trance" classic, "3 A.M. Eternal", and made it more fitting for the charts. This version, subtitled "Live at the S.S.L." after the Solid State Logic mixing board which they recorded it on, was released as a single in January 1991. Upon release, the new direction again proved a success, as the track gave the group their second number one in the UK. Shortly afterwards, in March 1991, the album was released. The album was a success, reaching no. 3 on the UK albums chart. But, of course, they were nowhere near done. In April, they released a new version of "Last Train to Trancentral", its final form, which matched the stadium house versions of its Pure Trance friends, unlike the version on the LP which stuck mainly to its Pure Trance roots. This single reached no. 2 on the UK singles chart.

With the three of their Pure Trance singles remixed and stadium house-ified, was that it? No. Because, while they had been massively successful in the UK, there was somewhere they hadn't got to yet: America. "America: What Time is Love" is the third and final incarnation of the classic KLF track. And, to go from fact to opinion, briefly, it was given a final version worth the genre-creating original, because this final version, released in September 1991 in the US exclusively, is to me one of the greatest pieces of electronic music ever created. The single was later released in the UK in February 1992, with the B-side "America No More" and a slightly extended "uncensored" version of the original. Released earlier in the UK was a promo release called "America: What Time is January?", which is now a collectors item.

But before that, they dredged back from hell their old alias, The Justified Ancients of Mu Mu, to show their fans they hadn't gone all soft and commercial. Back in late 1990, the group released a now-collectors item promo single on grey-coloured vinyl, "It's Grim Up North". They'd been hinting at it for ages, with their Top of the Pops performances of "What Time is Love" and "3 A.M. Eternal" featuring the phrase on their shirts and on rapper Ricardo Da Force's, respectively. In October 1991, they finally released the track fully, in a new form, under the Justified Ancients of Mu Mu name. Both the original and the new version are extremely heavy techno tracks, and fights still break out on Discogs over which is heavier, with it usually ending in "You had to be there! YouTube doesn't do it justice!".

Then, now they had proven to their fans they weren't softies, they released their most commercial song yet. On their first album as the JAMs, the group had created their "national anthem", if you will. The (extremely cheesy) lyrics of "Justified & Ancient" were featured a lot in the group's works, including in Chill Out, and in The White Room. The group decided to put this song out as a single, and while working on it, they found the original vocals they had recorded back in 1987 weren't working, and Cauty half-jokingly suggested, "What this needs is Tammy Wynette". Wynette was a country singer who hadn't entered the UK top 40 since 1976. Drummond, who was a huge fan of country and had all of Wynette's records, decided to pursue this idea, and within 30 minutes he was on the phone with Wynette herself, who agreed. And so, the KLF's next single, "Justified & Ancient", featuring Tammy Wynette, was released in late November 1991. And as any chart aficionado would recognise, late November only means one thing. This lot were aiming for the Christmas number one spot. It's a tough competition every year, but given their past record, they could do it. But, there was just one problem, and to put it in the… kind words of Drummond, "and then Freddie Mercury goes and dies…". Yes, Freddie Mercury, the frontman of Queen (which if you didn't know, you need to have your tongue checked for any sign of taste buds), died literally the day before the single was released. And his band, following this, released a charity single for AIDS prevention on the 9 December. And with that, the KLF's chances were all out. The charity single stuck to the top of the charts for five weeks, blocking the KLF from reaching the top, as well as some up-and-comers called "The Prodigy", whoever they are. Even so, on the sacred Christmas week, the single was fourth. Yes, the following two weeks it was at number two behind Queen, but even if Mercury hadn't died, they wouldn't have made it in time. And with no more songs to recycle, and far too tired and burnt out to write new ones, the KLF decided what was to happen next. The group had always been about rebellion, even since the Justified Ancients of Mu Mu days, but now they had become the status quo. They were the best-selling singles artist of 1991 in the UK, as commercial as you can get really, and so their final act of rebellion had to be to betray the entire record-buying public. This was it: the end.

Oh, did you think that was the end? You must be mistaken, this is the KLF we're talking about! You really think they'd go out without a bang? Well, they certainly didn't. You see, in late 1991, Drummond was sat in the bath when he heard something on the radio. A Peel session, like the one Cauty had recorded with the Orb, but not featuring the ambience of their kind of music, but instead a genre of music known as "crust", being performed by a band called "Extreme Noise Terror". That should give you an idea of what "crust" is, but if you don't get it, it's a form of heavy metal. Drummond liked it, and wanted to collaborate with the band. It took some convincing, but they agreed. They began to work together in the studio, on a project called The Black Room. This project had been mentioned even before the release of The White Room, and was, as the name implies, intended to be the "yang to the yin of The White Room." At first it was going to sound like "It's Grim Up North", then "America: What Time is Love", and now, in its third form, to sound like… uh, whatever those sessions produced. But remember, the KLF were going to end it all. They didn't realise this at the start of these sessions, but by the time they had decided, there was no album yet. The Black Room never came to fruition, but one thing from its sessions did. In December 1991, the BBC asked the KLF if they wanted to appear to perform "3 A.M. Eternal" on the special Christmas edition of their show Top of the Pops. On TOTP, the BBC will often ask its performers to lip-sync, to the annoyance of many bands, such as Nirvana and Queen. The KLF were perfectly happy with it, and sent the track they were to lip-sync over to the BBC. However, upon listening, the BBC discovered it wasn't the "stadium house" the band was known for, no, this was a new version of "3 A.M. Eternal", recorded with Extreme Noise Terror. Hearing it, the BBC decided to not allow the KLF to play. The recording they had sent to TOTP was later released on a 7" single, but only through a mailing list. This would prove to be the last release before the KLF's end. However, the BBC obviously didn't alert anyone else about the KLF's new collaboration, as the KLF were then booked to perform at that year's BRIT Awards, broadcasted on ITV. Live. No backing track.

The KLF now had a perfect opportunity to, effectively, commit commercial suicide live on stage. The band began to plot how they were to do this. Their first idea was to disembowel a sheep on stage, and throw blood onto the audience. Why? Because the sheep was the symbol of the KLF, so it would be as close as they could get to actually killing themselves on stage. However, they had a problem. That being that the heavy metal, hardcore crust band they were performing with, Extreme Noise Terror, were vegetarians. They said they wouldn't play if the plan were to go ahead, and so the idea was struck down, even though the dead sheep had already been bought. Drummond considered cutting his hand off with an axe in the performance, but felt it was too extreme. Instead, they decided to perform the song as normal, but at the end, have Drummond fire blanks from a machine gun over the audience. And that they did. In front of an audience of pop listeners and celebrities, they performed a crust version of their number one hit, and left the stage to the sound of their promoter saying over the PA system, "The KLF have now left the music business". No one took it seriously, until a few months later when their entire catalogue was removed from sale. The last thing the band put out, although not music, was the dead sheep they had bought; it was dumped outside one of the afterparties of the award show. Written on a message tied across it, "I died for you (bon appetit)". With their catalogue deleted, they announced, "For the foreseeable future there will be no further record releases from The Justified Ancients of Mu Mu, The Timelords, The KLF and any other past, present and future name attached to our activities. As of now all our past releases are deleted … If we meet further along be prepared … our disguise may be complete."

And now, we reach today. Where will the KLF go next? Who knows. Maybe a compilation of the Pure Trance originals, with the unreleased 3rd and 4th editions? A final cut or remaster of the film? Possibly even new music? Time will tell. Whatever it is, the mythos of the KLF will always be, to me, the most impressive feat in the history of music. Becoming the biggest selling singles artist of the year, while making music in a squat in south London, and most importantly while making the press ever more confused by their presence. The KLF were the ultimate rebellion against the entire music industry, and are the only band I've ever heard of to publicly and intentionally destroy their own careers. Legends until the end.
