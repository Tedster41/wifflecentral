The KLF - Chill Out

The tale of the KLF is a long one, filled with unreleased tracks and remixes, rumours of this and that, and no real answers from the actual guys behind the band. There is a minefield of unreleased KLF tracks, and whether they're real or not is down to how gullible you are. Luckily, we won't be talking about most of that here, as this album is really part of a different story. You see, the KLF consists of two guys: Bill Drummond and Jimmy Cauty. Cauty is famous not just for being a member of the KLF, but also for founding another band by the name of the Orb. The Orb, unlike the KLF, are still around today, producing new music. However, it's not Cauty making it. The question of why not involves this album. Chill Out is regarded as one of the best ambient albums: albums with bare instrumentation, particularly percussion-wise, and mainly instead focus on creating a calming experience where you can, well, chill out. Cauty and the other member of the Orb, Alex Paterson, began doing this in special nights in nightclubs, where clubbers could go in refuge of the drum-heavy acid house dancefloors of 1989. They would combine lots of samples of other songs, BBC sound effects CDs and old science fiction movies to create a flowing experience of sound for hours on end, a perfect accompaniment to many kinds of drugs. But, did they ever get these performances down on tape? Well allegedly, this is one. Cauty and Paterson broke up in 1990, as a result of label disputes. Paterson kept the Orb name, while Cauty went back to the KLF, allegedly with the Orb's tapes. Cauty released a solo album later in 1990, which was originally intended to be the Orb's first album, titled Space. Earlier that year, Chill Out was released, and whether this was an old Orb project is debated, although the answer seems to be leaning in the Orb direction. Paterson and two independent journalists have alleged that Chill Out was a creation of Paterson that Cauty stole and released under the KLF name. The album's notes claim it was "composed, compiled and collated by the KLF". Even so, Chill Out isn't the only ambient set that the KLF have released. That's right, we're not talking about B-sides anymore, we're talking about whole other sets.

Let's start with a set known as Waiting. Waiting is actually a film the KLF made, one of multiple films that the band produced. The film, which was completed in 1990, is a little over 40 minutes long, and consists only of footage filmed by the band on the Scottish island of Jura, which they obviously grew affectionate towards as it would later be where they'd burn one million pounds. Yeah, the KLF were very weird. Anyway, the film itself is… something (believe me, I watched the whole thing), but as the band themselves admit, it consists entirely of just them "waiting". The soundtrack to this film is sort of like a darker version of Chill Out. Now, whether or not we just put the soundtrack on a CD (as was done in 1997 on Waiting for the Rites of Mu, a release which is very debatably official) or put the film on a DVD is a tricky question, but I've opted for the CD option because it feels easier. And considering it's over 40 minutes (and so is the next thing), this disc will just consist of one song, "Waiting".

CD 2: Waiting

  1. "Waiting" – 42:39

Now, you heard me right, the next thing is also over 40 minutes. It, unlike Waiting, has little known about it, and has only been released unofficially. Its title? "Made @ 3 A.M. Coming Down Off E". Sounds pretty self-explanatory. Like I said, we don't know much (other than the title) about how it came to be, and our best guess is that it was made around the same time as Chill Out. It was first bootlegged in 1999, however, so confirming that is difficult. Even so, it is still an ambient mix, so I'd say it counts. Once again, the next track is too long to fit on a CD with this one, so CD 3 will have to just be this track and nothing else.

CD 3: "Made @ 3 A.M. Coming Down Off E

  1. ""Made @ 3 A.M. Coming Down Off E" – 45:17

Yep. There's still another 40 minute set, but this is the last one, I promise. The KLF disbanded in 1992, for various reasons, which again we're not going to get into right now. After various ventures, some together, some apart, the two re-released their catalogue on streaming services, including Come Down Dawn, a new version of Chill Out exclusively for streaming services in February 2021. It is effectively the original but without unlicensed samples that they could not get the rights to (for example "In the Ghetto" by Elvis Presley). Since this version has been altered quite significantly, I think it's worth including.

But, we're not done yet! We haven't actually spoken about the (kind of) single from this album, "Madrugada Eterna". Featuring Graham Lee on pedal steel guitar, this sweet seven minute track is definitely the most memorable (and transcendental) part of Chill Out, and has multiple versions aside from the one on the album. The first is the Club Mix, released officially on some vinyl promos in 1990. The second is another version I can't find too much information about, and can't confirm if it's a remix done by the KLF, but the "Wolf in the Jungle" version of the track has been included on a few bootleg compilations of the KLF's work. I'm including it just in case it is real. And the final version of this track to be featured is an infamous version of the track, which has no proper name, but is commonly known as the "303 Edit" or "303 Mix". It was featured as the soundtrack to a two-minute trailer to the KLF's film The White Room, which never got released because the project was too ambitious, and the band didn't have the money to make it. However, this "303" version of the track has become so renowned in the community because it's so good! And worse, we still haven't been able to find a version longer than the two minute cut featured in the trailer. Longer fan-made versions have been made, which I recommend you check out, but I can't include them here, so I will instead just use the two minute version. The final inclusion is a remix of the KLF's signature song, "What Time is Love", known as the Virtual Reality Mix. This is included because snippets of the original track were included in some of the sets we've mentioned today, and this mix bears a similar resemblance to those sections.
